Where This Fits

Part 1: The Human OS

Why social engineering works — every time, on everybody

Why SE Works

Your brain runs on shortcuts. Most of the time that's a feature — you don't consciously analyze every face, every email, every request. Social engineers abuse the shortcuts.

Cognitive Load

Busy, tired, distracted people skip verification steps. Attackers pick busy times on purpose.

Trust Defaults

We're wired to trust by default. Paranoia is exhausting; society runs on assumed honesty.

Emotion > Logic

Fear, urgency, excitement, curiosity — strong emotions bypass the part of your brain that asks "wait, is this real?"

"I could go into any office building, and 30 minutes later walk out with anything I wanted. I never hacked a computer first — I hacked the person."

— Kevin Mitnick, The Art of Deception

Cialdini's Six Principles

Psychologist Robert Cialdini spent 30+ years studying how salespeople, cult leaders, and con artists got people to say yes. He found six repeatable patterns. Every social-engineering attack uses at least one.

Reciprocity

Do something for me, I feel I owe you.

Commitment

Once I say yes to a small thing, I want to stay consistent.

Social Proof

If everyone else is doing it, it must be okay.

Authority

I defer to people who look in charge.

Liking

I say yes to people I like or find attractive.

Scarcity

If it's rare or expiring, I want it more.

Cialdini in the Wild

You see these every day. Once you can name them, you can resist them.

"Here's a free demo — now buy the full version" — reciprocity. You got something, now you feel obligated.

"You already said you care about security — so click this link" — commitment. Hook you with a small agreement, escalate from there.

"142,000 people upgraded this month" — social proof. The herd is moving; you should move too.

"This is the CEO. I need a wire transfer by end of day" — authority. A title on a badge, a confident voice, and most people comply.

The attacker who smiles, remembers your dog's name, laughs at your joke — liking. Rapport is a weapon.

"Offer expires in 10 minutes" — scarcity. No time to check = no time to think.

Hadnagy's Additions

After running social-engineering engagements for over a decade, Hadnagy added three more levers he saw constantly in real attacks:

Urgency

Compresses your decision window. When you don't have time to think, you run on defaults — and defaults are exploitable.

Fear

"Your account will be suspended." "You are being sued." Fear triggers the amygdala; the prefrontal cortex (the part that questions) gets quieter.

Concession

Ask for something huge, then "settle" for what you actually wanted. Feels like a win to the victim — it was the plan all along.

Every scam text, phishing email, and vishing call stacks 2–4 of these levers on top of each other. Count them the next time you get one.

Part 2: OSINT

Open-Source Intelligence — you are already leaking

What Is OSINT?

Open-Source Intelligence is information collected from public sources. No hacking. No illegal access. Just looking — carefully, systematically, at what is already out there.

Passive OSINT

Collection without touching the target — reading public social media, Google searches, news articles, archived pages. Leaves no trace.

Active OSINT

Any interaction with the target or their systems — visiting their website, pinging a server, sending a friend request, DM-ing an employee. Can be detected.

"Information gathering is the most important step in the SE process. Do it right and the rest of the engagement is half done."

— Chris Hadnagy, Social Engineering: The Science of Human Hacking

What's Leaking About You?

Before we profile anyone else — think about your own footprint. An attacker targeting you as a stepping stone to your parents' company, or to take over your accounts, has this at minimum:

Instagram / TikTok

Real name, face, friends, school, routines, the inside of your house, your pet's name (very often your security-question answer).

Discord / Gaming

Username reuse links gaming identity to real identity. Server lists reveal interests — perfect for pretexting.

Data Breaches

If you reuse passwords, a breach of one site hands attackers access to all of them. Check haveibeenpwned.com.

Family & Friends

Your mom's maiden name, your sibling's birthday, your school mascot — all public, all used as "security" questions.

Go home tonight and Google yourself. Most of you will find more than you expected.

Google Dorking

Google is an OSINT tool if you know its query language. These operators turn the search bar into a precision instrument:

site:example.com

Results only from that domain. Scope a single target.

filetype:pdf

Only documents of that type. Great for leaked internal files.

intitle:"index of"

Exposed directory listings — unsecured file servers.

inurl:login

URLs containing "login" — find admin panels.

"John Smith" site:linkedin.com

Exact-phrase name search on one site. Profile enumeration.

-careers site:target.com

Exclude results with a word. Filter noise.

There is a public database of useful queries called the Google Hacking Database (GHDB), maintained by Offensive Security.

Username Enumeration

Most people reuse handles. Find one username, find dozens of accounts — each one a piece of the puzzle.

The technique

Check a candidate username against hundreds of platforms at once — Twitter, GitHub, Reddit, Steam, Twitch, Venmo, forums. Match profiles across sites to build a pattern-of-life.

Public tools

whatsmyname.app and sherlock (open-source CLI) both check hundreds of sites in seconds. Investigators and attackers both use them.

Why it matters

The GitHub profile reveals your employer. The Strava reveals your running route. The old MySpace reveals your 2009 security questions. Separate identities don't stay separate.

Images Leak More Than You Think

Reverse Image Search

Google Lens, TinEye, Yandex. Drop a profile pic in and see everywhere else it appears — old blogs, alt accounts, dating sites. Kills the idea of a "throwaway" photo.

EXIF Metadata

Camera model, timestamp, sometimes GPS coordinates baked into the image file. Most social platforms strip it, but many forums, Discord, and raw email attachments do not.

Background Clues

Window reflections. Street signs. Delivery boxes with addresses. A license plate in a side mirror. OSINT analysts train specifically on this — so do stalkers.

The famous case

In 2019, a Japanese stalker identified a pop star's home by zooming in on the reflection in her eye from a selfie and matching it to a train station. Your photos are maps.

Profiling a Target: A Walkthrough

Pretend we are red-teamers hired to test a fictional company, Acme Logistics. Goal: get payroll redirected to our account via a believable impersonation of their CFO.

1

LinkedIn: CFO is Jane Doe. Scraped her "About" blurb, 14 years at the company, MBA from State U, mentions her two daughters.

2

Company website: press release names the new Controller — Jane's direct report, the person who can actually authorize a wire transfer.

3

Instagram (public): Jane posted from Maui yesterday. She is unreachable, which is exactly when CFO-impersonation attacks happen.

4

Earnings call transcript: Jane says "team" constantly, uses the phrase "let's circle back," signs emails with just "J." Tone is copyable.

5

Data breach lookup: Jane's personal email was in a 2019 breach. Her old password was "Maui2015!" — gives us a hint about her password habits.

What That OSINT Just Bought Us

Five public sources, maybe 30 minutes of work. Here is what we now know well enough to weaponize:

Who to impersonate

Jane Doe, CFO — confirmed offline this week.

Who to target

The Controller — has wire-transfer authority and reports to Jane.

The pretext

"I'm on vacation with bad reception, urgent wire needed before the weekend."

Tone & signature

Copy Jane's actual phrasing. Sign "J."

Emotional levers

Authority (CFO), urgency (before weekend), liking (friendly tone).

Plausible deniability

Vacation explains short, typo-ish emails and a non-corporate phone.

This is how real BEC attacks start. The "Nigerian prince" is dead — the modern version is a researched, precise impersonation.

Part 3: Pretexting

The cover story — who you are, why you're asking

What Is a Pretext?

A pretext is the invented identity, role, or reason an attacker uses to justify their request. It answers the target's unspoken question: "Why are you talking to me?"

A weak pretext

"Hi, this is IT. I need your password."

Too generic. No name, no context, no legitimate reason. Triggers alarm in anyone paying attention.

A strong pretext

"Hey, Dana from the Okta rollout team — I have you on the list for the MFA push pilot. Mark said he'd ping you, has he? Want me to walk you through enrolling real quick?"

Specific name, named project, named internal person, helpful framing. Most people would comply.

What Makes a Pretext Believable

Specificity

Named people, named systems, named projects. Vague requests from "IT" die; requests from "Dana on the Okta rollout" live.

Consistency

Story doesn't contradict itself. Names match between the first email and the follow-up call. Details from OSINT line up.

Congruence

Tone matches role. "IT helpdesk" sounds casual and slightly harried. "Legal" sounds formal and precise. Mismatch = tell.

Legitimate Reason

Why you, why now, why this channel? A good pretext has clean answers for all three.

Rehearsal

Hadnagy's rule: the more of the pretext that is actually true, the less there is to remember and the less there is to get caught on.

Stay in Character

Hadnagy teaches pretexting as method acting. The pretext is not a lie you recite — it's a role you inhabit.

Build a full persona

Name, title, office location, manager's name, weekend plans. You will be asked small-talk questions.

Dress the voice

If you're "busy IT," you sound slightly rushed. If you're "curious new hire," you ask a dumb question first. Match the role's energy.

Prep the escape hatch

If confronted, a pro has a plan — apologize, blame a confusion, disengage. Amateurs freeze and get caught.

Don't over-explain

Guilty people over-explain. Real coworkers don't justify every request. A confident "Can you run this for me?" beats a nervous paragraph.

"The best pretexts are 90% truth. That way you don't have to remember what you said."

— Chris Hadnagy

Case Study: Kevin Mitnick & Motorola

Kevin Mitnick (1963–2023) is the most famous social engineer in history. In his memoir Ghost in the Wires, he describes how he got the source code for a new Motorola flip phone — entirely through pretexting.

Step 1

Called Motorola's main number. Asked for the group that worked on the new phone. Got transferred around — each transfer gave him a new name to drop.

Step 2

Posed as an internal engineer who "lost access" and needed the source code sent to a test FTP server. Used the real names he'd collected.

Step 3

Target engineer hesitated — needed a manager sign-off. Mitnick provided the manager's name (just collected), the manager's extension, and called the manager himself with a different pretext to get the sign-off.

Step 4

Source code was sent. No malware, no exploit, no zero-day. Three phone calls.

The lesson: pretexts compound. Each successful call gave Mitnick more ammunition for the next one.

Red Flags That Blow a Pretext

These are the same flags defenders use to catch attackers — and the same mistakes attackers train themselves to avoid.

Unusual channel

"My boss texted me from a number I've never seen."

Unusual urgency

"Why does this need to happen in the next five minutes?"

Over-familiarity too fast

First contact and they already know your kid's name. Rapport on rails.

Resistance to verification

"I can't wait for you to call back." Real people can wait.

Wrong details

Wrong manager name, wrong team acronym, slightly wrong terminology. Inside knowledge is not a perfect mask.

Secrecy request

"Don't tell anyone about this transaction." Legitimate work rarely requires you to hide it.

Part 4: Elicitation

Getting information without asking for it

Elicitation ≠ Interrogation

Elicitation is the art of getting people to tell you things they'd never tell you if you asked outright. It feels like conversation to the target. That's the point.

Interrogation

  • Direct questions
  • Obvious agenda
  • Target is on guard
  • Recorded, memorable

Elicitation

  • Statements, not questions
  • Agenda invisible
  • Target is relaxed
  • Forgettable — "just a chat"

The FBI publishes a public brochure on elicitation because foreign-intelligence officers use these same techniques at trade shows and in bars.

Elicitation Techniques (1/2)

Flattery

"You must be the expert on this — I've been trying to figure out how your team does X." People love to be recognized. They'll explain proudly.

Deliberate False Statement

Say something confidently wrong. "I heard your team still uses SHA-1." The target's instinct is to correct you — and in correcting you, they hand you the real answer.

Assumed Knowledge

Speak as if you already know. "Once the migration to the new CRM finishes next month…" If there is a migration, the target rolls with it — and now you know there is one.

Elicitation Techniques (2/2)

Quid Pro Quo

Offer information first. "At my last job we used Okta and had constant problems with…" The target reciprocates with their own setup, their own pain points.

Sounding Board

Complain about a problem related to their work. People love to give advice. Advice about how their org solved it is a detailed map of their systems.

Feigned Ignorance

"I'm new, can you walk me through how access requests work around here?" Makes the target the teacher. Teachers over-share.

Strategic Silence

Ask a small question and then don't fill the pause. Silence is uncomfortable. Most people will keep talking to fill it — often with exactly what you wanted.

Case Study: DEF CON SECTF

Hadnagy founded the Social Engineering Capture the Flag contest at DEF CON. Competitors sit in a glass booth and cold-call a pre-assigned Fortune 500 company. They have 25 minutes, a list of "flags" (pieces of info), and a script they can deviate from.

Typical flags

Browser version, OS, antivirus product, VPN vendor, badge system, cafeteria vendor, dumpster pickup day.

Typical pretext

"Marketing research," "new-employee benefits," "facilities compliance audit."

The disturbing finding

Companies with sophisticated technical defenses — banks, defense contractors, hospitals — almost all get most flags captured. Years of SECTF data published in the annual report.

Why

Employees are trained on phishing emails, not phone calls. The voice on the other end sounds friendly, sounds like someone who belongs, and people help.

Part 5: Attack Vectors

How the skills get delivered — with real cases

Phishing: Anatomy

Every phishing email has the same four parts. Learn to spot them and you'll catch almost all of them.

1. The Sender

Display name vs. real address. "Apple Support" <security@app1e-verify.com>. Hover, don't trust.

2. The Hook

Subject + opening line optimized for one of Hadnagy's levers — usually urgency or fear. "Your account will be locked in 24 hours."

3. The Payload

A link to a credential-harvesting page, an attachment with a malicious macro, or a reply-to address for data exfiltration.

4. The Call to Action

"Click here to verify." "Open the attached invoice." "Reply with your W-2." Always a single clear action, time-pressured.

Phishing Variants You'll See

Phishing

Mass email — low effort, low success rate, still works at scale.

Spear Phishing

Targeted at one person or small group. Uses OSINT. Much higher success rate.

Whaling

Spear phishing aimed at executives. Big payoff justifies research time.

Vishing

Voice phishing — phone calls. Helpdesks are favorite targets.

Smishing

SMS phishing. "Your package is delayed." "IRS owes you a refund."

Quishing

QR-code phishing. Fake codes in parking lots, posters, emails. Bypasses URL-filtering.

BEC

Business Email Compromise — impersonating execs for wire fraud. Billions lost per year.

Deepfake Voice / Video

AI-cloned voice on a call, AI-generated face on a video conference. 2024 saw the first nine-figure losses.

Case: MGM Resorts (September 2023)

Attackers from the group Scattered Spider took down MGM's casinos, hotels, slot machines, digital room keys, and reservation systems across Las Vegas for days. Estimated loss: $100M+.

1

OSINT on LinkedIn — identified an MGM IT employee by name and title.

2

10-minute phone call to MGM's IT helpdesk, posing as that employee, claiming they were locked out.

3

Helpdesk reset MFA for the attacker — no callback verification, no second channel.

4

From there: access to the Okta admin, then deployed ransomware across the entire environment.

Every layer of MGM's technical security was intact. A phone call beat all of it.

Case: Twilio (August 2022)

Twilio is the SMS backbone for thousands of services — including many MFA providers. The attacker group 0ktapus ran a smishing campaign against Twilio employees. Roughly 130 downstream companies were compromised through the blast radius.

The text

"Your Twilio schedule has changed, please click here to review." Link went to a near-pixel-perfect Okta login page on a domain like twilio-sso.com.

The trick

The fake page relayed credentials and the MFA code to the attacker in real time — classic Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM). MFA did not save them.

The blast radius

Once inside Twilio, attackers pivoted into customer accounts including Signal — exposing phone numbers for ~1,900 Signal users.

Case: Twitter (July 2020)

A then-17-year-old named Graham Ivan Clark and two accomplices hijacked the verified accounts of Elon Musk, Joe Biden, Barack Obama, Bill Gates, Apple, Uber, and others — and posted a Bitcoin-doubling scam. Netted ~$120K in a few hours.

1

Phone spear phishing of Twitter employees working from home during COVID.

2

Posed as Twitter IT, said the VPN was broken and sent employees to a fake single-sign-on page.

3

Stole live credentials + MFA codes — got into the internal admin tool that could reset any account's email.

4

Took over ~130 accounts, posted scam tweets, were arrested within two weeks — FBI traced Bitcoin and Discord handles back to real names.

A high-schooler shut down one of the world's largest tech companies for half a day with a phone. The lead attacker got three years in prison.

Case: Arup Deepfake (February 2024)

Engineering firm Arup (the people who designed the Sydney Opera House) lost $25 million in a single wire transfer to attackers using deepfaked video of the company's CFO.

Setup

A finance employee in Hong Kong got an email from "the CFO" asking to discuss a confidential transaction. Classic BEC start — but this time, the employee asked for a video call to verify.

The call

A video conference with the CFO and several other colleagues. All of them were deepfakes, generated from publicly available video footage of the real executives. Voices, faces, mannerisms — all synthetic.

The lesson

"Verify over a second channel" used to mean "get them on video." That heuristic is now dead. Verification means a known-good phone number, not a number they give you on the call.

The Common Thread

MGM. Twilio. Twitter. Arup. Four different vectors, four different years, same playbook:

1

OSINT to identify the right person — and the right moment.

2

A pretext tuned to the target's role and current context.

3

A channel chosen to minimize verification — phone, SMS, deepfake video.

4

At least two Cialdini/Hadnagy levers stacked — typically authority + urgency.

5

A technical pivot once access is obtained (MFA reset, session hijack, wire transfer).

The technology changes. The playbook does not.

Part 6: Defense

Protecting yourself and the people you love

The Red-Flag Checklist

If a message or call triggers two or more of these, stop and verify before doing anything:

Unexpected and urgent

Asks for credentials, codes, or money

Uses a channel they'd normally never use

Links go to a domain that's almost right

Pressures you not to check with anyone

Triggers strong emotion (fear, excitement, guilt)

Breaks normal process ("skip the ticket this once")

Something small is off — and you noticed

The Callback Rule

Single most effective defense against SE, worldwide, across every case we've looked at:

Verify through an independent channel using a number or address you already had — never one provided in the suspicious message.

Correct

Bank texts that something's wrong. You hang up and call the number on the back of your card.

Wrong

Bank texts that something's wrong. You call the number in the text. That number goes straight to the attacker.

Same rule for video calls after the Arup attack — a deepfaked face can say "call me back at this number." The number is the attacker.

Slow Down. That's the Whole Trick.

Every attack you just saw depended on the target not pausing. If you pause, the attack usually dies.

30-second rule

Before clicking, wiring, or sharing, count to 30 and ask: "Why is this urgent, really? Who benefits if I do this fast?"

Second pair of eyes

Show the message to someone. Attackers count on you feeling embarrassed or confidential. Break that and you break the attack.

"I'll get back to you"

Magic phrase. Real requests survive a 10-minute delay. Fake requests escalate, threaten, or disappear.

Protecting People Who Don't Know This Stuff

Your parents, grandparents, and younger siblings are prime targets. In 2023, the FBI IC3 reported $3.4 billion lost by people over 60 to online fraud. You are their best defense.

Teach the callback rule

One rule. Easy to remember. Save the real customer-service number for their bank in their phone today.

Agree on a family code word

Deepfake voice scams target grandparents: "I'm in jail, send money." A family safe-word defeats this instantly.

Review their texts together

Offer to look at weird messages — no judgment. The goal is they'll ask you before clicking, not after.

Enable call-screening

iPhones: "Silence Unknown Callers." Android has similar. Most vishing calls die on voicemail.

If You Got Got

Everyone gets got eventually. Professionals in this field have fallen for things. The difference between a minor scare and a major breach is how fast you respond, not whether you were perfect.

1

Tell someone fast. Parent, teacher, IT, whoever is responsible for that account. Minutes matter — attackers move quickly once in.

2

Change the password on that account and any other account sharing it.

3

Turn on MFA if it wasn't already. Revoke active sessions.

4

Report. For US-based fraud: ic3.gov (FBI) and reportfraud.ftc.gov. If money moved: call your bank immediately.

5

Don't hide it out of shame. Shame is the attacker's last weapon. Telling someone is how the attack ends.

Part 7: Ethics & the Law

Where this knowledge is legal — and where it isn't

The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act

The CFAA (18 U.S.C. § 1030) is the main US federal law governing unauthorized computer access. Social engineering is not a loophole — tricking someone into giving you access is unauthorized access under the law.

What it covers

Accessing a "protected computer" without authorization, or exceeding authorized access. "Protected computer" has been interpreted broadly — almost anything connected to the internet counts.

Penalties

Range from misdemeanor to felony. The Twitter 2020 hacker got 3 years. Scattered Spider members (MGM) face decades if convicted.

State laws stack

Every state has additional computer crime laws. Identity theft, wire fraud, impersonation — each can be charged separately. Penalties compound.

Authorization Is the Entire Difference

Professional red-teamers and social engineers use every technique in this deck — legally. The difference is one document.

Pentester / red-teamer

  • Signed Statement of Work
  • Explicit scope (what's allowed)
  • Rules of Engagement (what's off-limits)
  • "Get out of jail" letter on paper
  • Emergency contact at the client

Everyone else doing the same thing

  • Committing federal crimes
  • Subject to CFAA + state laws
  • Subject to civil suits
  • No "I was just curious" defense
  • Permanent record, career-ending

Practicing on your friends "just to see if it works" is not a gray area. Get consent every time, even for experiments.

Hadnagy's Code of Ethics

Social-Engineer LLC publishes a Code of Ethics for the profession. The key principles:

Leave people better than you found them. A successful engagement teaches, it doesn't humiliate.

Never use fear, threats, or emotional manipulation that damages someone. Urgency is fine; psychological harm is not.

Stay in scope. Don't chase an interesting tangent outside what's authorized.

Protect the information you obtain. You saw the data because the job required it, not because it's yours.

Report honestly. Don't inflate findings. Don't hide mistakes.

Where This Becomes a Job

If any of this clicked for you, it's also a career path — one of the most in-demand in cybersecurity. Salaries typically run $80K–$200K+ depending on role and seniority.

Penetration Tester

Break in, write it up, help fix it. Social-engineering engagements are a standard part of the work.

Red-Team Operator

Longer, more realistic attack simulations. SE is often the opening move.

Dedicated SE Specialist

Niche. Firms like Social-Engineer LLC, TrustedSec, and many consulting shops staff this role directly.

Security Awareness Engineer

Build phishing simulations, training content, culture programs. Demand is exploding.

Threat Intelligence Analyst

OSINT is the core skill. Track threat actors, profile campaigns, brief defenders.

Incident Response / SOC

The other side of the fight. Recognize SE in your logs, respond, contain, lessons-learned.

Getting Started (Legally)

Read

Social Engineering: The Science of Human Hacking (Hadnagy). The Art of Deception (Mitnick). Influence (Cialdini). Ghost in the Wires (Mitnick).

Practice OSINT on yourself

Hunt your own public footprint. Fix what you find. This alone teaches most of Part 2 of this deck.

Compete legally

CTFs with OSINT and SE categories — TraceLabs missing-persons CTF, PicoCTF (student-friendly), HackTheBox.

Certify (eventually)

Security+ you're already doing. Then consider: OSCP (hands-on pentest), PNPT (TCM Security — includes SE), Hadnagy's own SEPP/SEPT courses.

Get authorized targets

HackTheBox, TryHackMe, and bug-bounty programs like HackerOne let you practice on real systems with written authorization.

Wrap-Up

What to take with you

Key Takeaways

1

SE works because of how human brains work, not because people are dumb. Cialdini's six + Hadnagy's urgency, fear, concession — count the levers in every scam.

2

OSINT is the foundation. Every serious attack starts with research. Your public footprint is your attack surface.

3

A strong pretext is specific, consistent, and 90% true. Weak pretexts die on the first challenge.

4

Elicitation is information-gathering through conversation. Statements, not questions. Silence is your best friend.

5

MGM, Twilio, Twitter, Arup all ran the same playbook: OSINT → pretext → low-verification channel → stacked levers → technical pivot.

6

The callback rule and slowing down beat almost every attack. Teach your family.

7

Authorization is the entire difference between a red-teamer and a felon. Same skills, one piece of paper.

Questions?

The human OS has no patches.

Training is the patch.
