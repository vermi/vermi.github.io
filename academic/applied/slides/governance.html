PART I

Security Governance

Structures, programs, and models for directing organizational security

What is Governance?

Definition

Governance programs are the sets of procedures and controls put in place to allow an organization to effectively direct its work. They coordinate the development and execution of strategic plans at all levels.

Why It Matters

  • Without governance, thousands of employees make independent decisions — chaos
  • Ensures every aspect of an organization’s work aligns with its strategy and goals
  • Policy serves as the primary governance tool for cybersecurity programs

Organizations often base policies on frameworks from NIST and ISO, and are further shaped by external compliance obligations from regulators.

Corporate Governance Model

The Hierarchy

Shareholders → elect → Board of Directors → hire → CEO → manage → Management Team

Board of Directors

  • Ultimate authority as owners’ representatives
  • Meet monthly or quarterly
  • Best practice: majority should be independent directors

CEO & Management

  • CEO handles day-to-day operations
  • Delegates to executives (CFO, COO, CISO)
  • Governance cascades downward through the hierarchy

Governance, Risk & Compliance (GRC)

GRC programs integrate three related tasks into a unified approach:

Governance

Setting strategic direction and ensuring alignment across the organization

Risk Management

Identifying, assessing, and mitigating threats to organizational objectives

Compliance

Adhering to laws, regulations, standards, and internal policies

These three functions are deeply interconnected. Governance sets the policies, risk management identifies what needs protecting, and compliance ensures the rules are followed.

Information Security Governance

The CISO’s Role

  • CEO delegates information security responsibility to the Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)
  • CISO works with peers to design and implement the security governance framework
  • CISO does not have operational control — must leverage management to enforce security

Key Responsibilities

  • Establish a management structure for the cybersecurity team
  • Create policies that apply to the entire organization
  • Use existing reporting channels and establish new ones as needed
  • Include escalation procedures for when the security team needs management support

The CISO and CEO must work together to ensure proper alignment of the information security program with corporate governance.

Centralized vs. Decentralized Governance

Centralized

  • Top-down approach
  • Central authority creates policies and standards
  • Enforced uniformly throughout the organization
  • Ensures consistency
  • May lack flexibility for unique business unit needs

Decentralized

  • Bottom-up approach
  • Individual business units are delegated authority
  • Units achieve cybersecurity objectives as they see fit
  • Greater flexibility and responsiveness
  • Risk of inconsistency and gaps

Exam Tip: Be able to distinguish between centralized and decentralized governance models. These topics come directly from the SY0-701 exam objectives.

Types of Governance Structures

Boards

Formal board of directors elected by shareholders (public companies) or members (nonprofits). Ultimate authority over the organization.

Committees

Internal committees consisting of subject matter experts (SMEs) and managers. May include security steering committees, change advisory boards, and audit committees.

Government Entities

Regulatory agencies play a role in governance of some organizations. Examples: banks regulated by the U.S. Treasury, healthcare by HHS, financial markets by SEC.

Real World: SolarWinds & CISO Accountability

What Happened

In October 2023, the SEC charged SolarWinds and its CISO Timothy Brown with fraud for allegedly overstating the company’s cybersecurity practices while concealing known risks — prior to and after the devastating 2020 supply chain attack.

Governance Lessons

  • CISOs are now personally accountable for security representations
  • Internal security assessments must align with external disclosures
  • Governance structures must ensure honest upward reporting
  • The board needs accurate security posture information to fulfill its oversight role

In July 2024, a federal judge dismissed most of the SEC’s claims but allowed the pre-breach fraud charge to proceed — reinforcing that misleading security statements have legal consequences.

PART II

Policy Framework

Policies, standards, procedures, guidelines, and exceptions

Policy Document Hierarchy

An organization’s information security policy framework generally includes four types of documents:

Policies

High-level statements of management intent. Compliance is mandatory.

Standards

Mandatory requirements describing how to carry out policies. More specific, may change more often.

Procedures

Detailed, step-by-step processes for specific circumstances. Compliance is mandatory.

Guidelines

Best practices and recommendations. Compliance is not mandatory.

Policies

Policies are high-level statements of management intent. They generally contain broad statements about cybersecurity objectives including:

  • Importance of cybersecurity to the organization
  • Requirements to protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability
  • Ownership of information created/possessed by the organization
  • Designation of the CISO as the responsible executive
  • Delegation of authority to create standards, procedures, and guidelines

Common Policy Documents

  • Information security policy — high-level authority and guidance
  • Acceptable use policy (AUP) — permissible uses of information resources
  • Incident response policy — how the org responds to security incidents
  • Business continuity / disaster recovery — operations during and after disasters
  • SDLC policy — security integrated into every stage of development
  • Change management policy — review, approve, and implement changes safely

Standards

Standards provide mandatory requirements describing how an organization will carry out its policies. They include specific configuration settings, required controls, and security objectives. Approved at a lower level than policies and may change more regularly.

Password Standards

Requirements for length, complexity, reuse, and expiration.

Access Control Standards

Account lifecycle from provisioning through decommissioning, including service accounts.

Physical Security Standards

Access control systems, surveillance, visitor policies, handling of physical security breaches.

Encryption Standards

Requirements for encrypting data in transit and at rest, algorithm selection, and key management.

Procedures

Procedures are detailed, step-by-step processes that individuals and organizations must follow in specific circumstances. Like checklists, they ensure a consistent process for achieving a security objective. Compliance is mandatory.

Common Procedures

  • Change management procedures — how to perform change management activities, including use of version control
  • Onboarding/offboarding procedures — how to add new user accounts when employees join and remove them when they leave
  • Playbooks — actions the incident response team takes for specific types of incidents

Example: Visa publishes What to Do if Compromised — a mandatory procedure requiring merchants to notify Visa within three days, engage a forensic investigator within five business days, and provide a final report within ten business days.

Guidelines

Definition

Guidelines provide best practices and recommendations related to a given concept, technology, or task. Compliance with guidelines is not mandatory — they offer helpful advice.

How They Fit

  • Policy sets high-level objectives and requires compliance with standards
  • Standards include details of required security controls
  • Guidelines provide advice to organizations seeking to comply with the policy and standards

The “optionality” of guidelines may vary by organizational culture. Some organizations treat guidelines almost as mandatory, while others view them as purely advisory.

External Considerations

When preparing policy documents, consider external factors that may impact your policies:

Regulatory

Legal requirements that mandate the use of certain controls (HIPAA, SOX, GLBA).

Legal

Laws at the national, state/regional, and local levels that affect operations.

Industry

Industry-specific considerations and best practices (PCI DSS for payment processing).

Jurisdictional

Global, national, and local/regional considerations based on where you operate (GDPR for EU data).

Failure to follow industry best practices may be seen as negligence and can cause legal liability for the organization.

Exceptions & Compensating Controls

Exception Process

When adopting security policies, organizations should also provide a mechanism for exceptions. An exception request should document:

  • Standard/requirement that requires an exception
  • Reason for noncompliance
  • Business and/or technical justification
  • Scope and duration of the exception
  • Risks associated with the exception
  • Compensating controls to mitigate risks
  • Plan for achieving compliance

Compensating Controls

Alternative means to achieve a security objective when the original control requirement cannot be met. Organizations should also develop remediation plans to bring the organization back into full compliance.

PCI DSS Compensating Control Criteria

PCI DSS includes one of the most formal compensating control processes. Five criteria must be met:

  1. Must meet the intent and rigor of the original requirement
  2. Must provide a similar level of defense as the original requirement
  3. Must be “above and beyond” other PCI DSS requirements
  4. Must address the additional risk imposed by not adhering to the original requirement
  5. Must address the requirement currently and in the future

Example: An organization must run an outdated OS because critical business software only works on it. As a compensating control, they isolate the system on a dedicated network segment with no access to other systems.

Monitoring and Revision

Policy Monitoring

  • Regularly evaluate implementation and efficacy of policies
  • Use SIEM systems and periodic audits/assessments
  • Assess alignment with current security needs, regulatory requirements, and technology changes
  • Gather feedback from staff who implement the policies

Policy Revision

  • Update policies to address shortcomings and new challenges
  • Communicate revised policies to all relevant personnel
  • Provide training on changes if necessary
  • Regular monitoring and timely revision are crucial for maintaining a robust security posture

PART III

Change Management & Personnel

Managing changes, people, and third-party relationships

Why Change Management Matters

The Problem

Changes often create unintended side effects that cause outages. A well-meaning administrator can change one system to resolve a problem but unknowingly cause a problem in other systems.

The Firewall Example

A firewall admin sees an unrecognized open port on Firewall 2 and closes it for security. But that port was needed for the web server to communicate with the database server. The result: cascading outage, hours of troubleshooting, finger-pointing — all from one unauthorized change.

Two Security Impacts

  • Availability: Unauthorized changes can directly cause outages (CIA triad)
  • Security weakening: Frustrated admins might grant excessive permissions to “just make it work” — violating least privilege

Change Management Process

1. Request the Change

Personnel identify desired changes and submit a request (often via an internal ticketing system).

2. Review the Change

Experts from multiple areas perform a complete impact analysis in consultation with stakeholders. May involve a change advisory board (CAB).

3. Approve/Reject the Change

Based on review, approve or reject. May require creation of a rollback/backout plan.

4. Test the Change

Test in a nonproduction environment. Include test results in documentation.

5. Schedule & Implement

Schedule during maintenance windows (off-peak hours). Have a backout plan ready.

6. Document the Change

Update configuration management documentation so all parties are aware and the system can be rebuilt if needed.

Technical Impact of Changes

Organizations should involve a diverse set of technical stakeholders when evaluating changes. Key issues to consider:

  • Will the change require modifications to security controls (firewall rules, allow/deny lists)?
  • Will any business or technical activities need to be restricted during or after the change?
  • Will the change cause downtime for critical systems?
  • Will the change require restarting services or applications?
  • Does the change involve legacy applications that lack vendor support?
  • Have all possible dependencies been identified and documented?

Emergency changes may bypass the normal process (e.g., during an active attack), but the administrator must still document the changes afterward for review.

Version Control & Documentation

Version Control

  • Ensures developers and users have the latest versions
  • Uses labeling/numbering systems (1.0, 1.1, 2.0)
  • Differentiates software sets across machines and time
  • Allows rollback to previous known-good configurations
  • Critical for tracking changes over time to deployed software

Documentation

  • Identifies current configuration of systems
  • Records who is responsible for each system and its purpose
  • Lists all changes applied to the baseline
  • Stored in a formal configuration management system
  • Must be updated before closing out any change management task

Before closing a change, update all related documentation, diagrams, policies, and procedures to reflect the impact of the change.

Real World: CrowdStrike Outage (July 2024)

What Happened

On July 19, 2024, a faulty CrowdStrike Falcon sensor update caused an estimated 8.5 million Windows systems worldwide to crash with the “Blue Screen of Death.” Airlines, hospitals, banks, and emergency services were disrupted globally.

Change Management Failures

  • The update was pushed directly to production without adequate staged testing
  • No canary/phased rollout to catch issues before global deployment
  • A content validator tool had a bug that failed to detect the problematic update
  • Recovery required manual, physical intervention on each affected machine — no easy backout plan

Estimated financial impact: $5.4 billion in losses for Fortune 500 companies alone. A textbook example of why change management processes — testing, staged rollouts, and backout plans — exist.

Personnel Management: Least Privilege

The Principle

Individuals should be granted only the minimum set of permissions necessary to carry out their job functions. Simple in concept, challenging in practice.

Privilege Creep

Occurs when an employee moves between roles within the organization, accumulating new privileges but never having past privileges revoked. Over time, the employee has far more access than their current role requires.

Least privilege requires careful attention to the privileges necessary for specific jobs and ongoing attention to avoid security issues. Regular access reviews help combat privilege creep.

Separation of Duties & Two-Person Control

Separation of Duties

  • Takes two sensitive tasks and ensures no single person can perform both
  • Classic example: In accounting, one person creates a new vendor, a different person issues checks
  • Fraud now requires collusion between two employees, reducing risk

Two-Person Control

  • Similar to separation of duties but different
  • Requires the participation of two people to perform a single sensitive action
  • Example: Two key holders needed to open a vault, two admins needed to approve a privileged action

Key Difference: Separation of duties prevents one person from holding two different sensitive privileges. Two-person control requires two people to participate in one sensitive action.

Job Rotation & Mandatory Vacations

Job Rotation

  • Employees with sensitive roles are periodically moved to other positions
  • Many types of fraud require ongoing concealment activities
  • If rotated out, the employee may not be able to continue concealment
  • The replacement may discover the fraud themselves

Mandatory Vacations

  • Employees forced to take annual vacations of a week or more
  • Access privileges are revoked during the vacation period
  • Serves a similar purpose to job rotation — disrupts ongoing fraud
  • Common in financial services and other regulated industries

Clean Desk Policy

Protects confidentiality by requiring all papers and materials to be secured before an employee leaves their desk. Limits exposure of sensitive information.

Onboarding & Offboarding

Onboarding

  • Standardized process for new employees
  • Create user accounts and grant appropriate access
  • Conduct background checks to uncover past issues
  • Sign NDA (nondisclosure agreement)
  • Provide initial security awareness training
  • Issue equipment and credentials

Offboarding

  • Revoke all access and credentials promptly
  • Recover organization assets (laptop, badge, keys)
  • Conduct exit interview
  • Remind employee of ongoing NDA obligations
  • Disable accounts — don’t just change passwords
  • Ensure orderly transfer of knowledge and responsibilities

NDAs require employees to protect confidential information during and after employment. Organizations should periodically remind employees of their NDA responsibilities.

Social Media Policies

Why Organizations Need Them

  • Employee activity on social media may reflect positively or negatively on the organization
  • Social media analysis may include assessments of both personal and professional accounts
  • Employees may inadvertently disclose sensitive information
  • Social media is a primary vector for social engineering attacks and reconnaissance

Organizations should make their expectations and practices clear in a social media policy. This helps employees understand boundaries while protecting the organization’s interests.

Third-Party Risk Management

The Risk

Many risks facing an organization come from third-party organizations — vendors, suppliers, and business partners. These relationships create dependencies that can introduce security vulnerabilities into the organization.

Third-Party Risk Lifecycle

Selection → Assessment → Agreements → Monitoring → Wind-Down

Vendor Selection & Due Diligence

Due Diligence

Thoroughly vetting potential vendors to ensure they meet organizational standards:

  • Financial stability and business reputation
  • Quality of products or services
  • Compliance with relevant regulations
  • Security practices and data handling procedures

Conflict of Interest

A conflict of interest arises when a vendor has a competing interest that could influence their behavior against the organization’s best interests. This may require contractual clauses or a decision not to engage with the vendor at all.

Vendor Assessment

Penetration Testing

Authorized simulated attacks to identify vulnerabilities in the vendor’s systems.

Right-to-Audit Clause

Contract provision allowing the customer to conduct or commission audits on the vendor’s operations.

Evidence of Internal Audits

Request proof of the vendor’s internal controls, compliance, and risk management practices.

Independent Assessments

Third-party evaluations including certifications like ISO 27001 or SOC reports.

Supply Chain Analysis

Assess the vendor’s suppliers and understand interdependencies that could impact their delivery.

Questionnaires

Collect information on vendor practices regularly, focusing on security, data handling, and business continuity.

Vendor Agreements

MSA — Master Service Agreement

Umbrella contract for work over an extended period. Includes detailed security and privacy requirements.

WO/SOW — Work Order / Statement of Work

Project-specific details that reference the MSA. Created for each new engagement.

SLA — Service Level Agreement

Specifies conditions of service (availability, response time, data durability) and remedies if the vendor fails to meet them.

MOU — Memorandum of Understanding

Informal letter documenting aspects of a relationship. Common between internal business units.

MOA — Memorandum of Agreement

More formal than an MOU. Details roles, responsibilities, resource allocation, and performance metrics.

BPA — Business Partners Agreement

Agreement between organizations doing business together in a partnership (joint development, profit sharing).

NDA — Non-Disclosure Agreement

Protects confidential information shared between parties. Vendor agreements should include NDA terms.

Vendor Monitoring & Wind-Down

Rules of Engagement

Define boundaries: communication protocols, responsibilities, and issue resolution processes. Prevent misunderstandings and disputes.

Ongoing Monitoring Areas

  • Performance: KPIs that quantitatively measure vendor performance
  • Security: Monitor vendor’s security posture, check for breaches/incidents
  • Compliance: Ensure vendor maintains certifications and meets regulatory requirements
  • Financial: Evaluate vendor financial health for long-term viability

Winding Down Relationships

Plan for orderly transition when a vendor relationship ends or a product reaches end of life (EOL) or end of service life (EOSL). Both parties should follow specific steps documented in advance.

Real World: MOVEit Breach (2023)

What Happened

The Cl0p ransomware group exploited a zero-day SQL injection vulnerability in Progress Software’s MOVEit Transfer file-sharing tool. The attack compromised data from over 2,700 organizations and 95 million individuals worldwide.

Third-Party Risk Lessons

  • Most victims were not direct MOVEit customers — they were affected through their vendors (supply chain risk)
  • Organizations like the BBC, Shell, U.S. government agencies, and major universities were impacted
  • Highlights the importance of supply chain analysis and understanding who your vendors depend on
  • Right-to-audit clauses and vendor security assessments could have identified exposure

Your security is only as strong as your weakest vendor’s security. Third-party risk management is not optional.

PART IV

Compliance, Frameworks & Awareness

Laws, regulations, standard frameworks, and security training programs

Complying with Laws & Regulations

The Landscape

Legislators and regulators worldwide take a keen interest in cybersecurity. The EU has broad data protection regulation (GDPR), while the U.S. relies on a patchwork of regulations covering different industries and information categories.

Key Point

Always consult your organization’s legal counsel and subject matter experts when designing a compliance strategy. You’ll need to understand national, territory, and state laws that apply to your operations.

Key Compliance Requirements

HIPAA

Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act — security and privacy rules for healthcare providers, insurers, and clearinghouses.

PCI DSS

Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard — rules for storing, processing, and transmitting credit/debit card data. Not a law, but a contractual obligation.

GLBA

Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act — requires U.S. financial institutions to have a formal security program with a designated responsible individual.

SOX

Sarbanes–Oxley Act — financial records of publicly traded companies require strong IT system assurance.

GDPR

General Data Protection Regulation — security and privacy requirements for personal information of EU residents worldwide.

FERPA

Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act — security and privacy controls for student educational records at U.S. institutions.

Compliance Reporting

Internal Reporting

  • Regular reports to management and board
  • Highlights state of compliance
  • Identifies gaps and recommends improvements
  • Helps allocate resources effectively
  • Ensures alignment with strategic goals

External Reporting

  • Mandated by regulatory bodies or contractual obligations
  • Provide documentation and evidence to external entities
  • Examples: PCI SSC compliance reports, GDPR evidence to DPAs
  • Crucial for maintaining good standing and avoiding penalties
  • Builds trust with customers and partners

Consequences of Noncompliance

Fines & Sanctions

Regulatory bodies can levy significant fines. Under GDPR: up to 4% of annual global turnover or €20 million. Nonfinancial sanctions may include restrictions on business operations.

Reputational Damage

Public disclosure of noncompliance or breaches severely tarnishes brand image. Customers and partners may lose trust and take their business elsewhere.

Loss of License

Regulatory authorities may suspend or revoke licenses critical to operations. Example: a bank losing its banking license for anti-money-laundering failures.

Contractual Impacts

Noncompliance can lead to contract termination, lost revenue, and costs of finding new partners. May also lead to lawsuits from affected parties.

Real World: Meta’s $1.3 Billion GDPR Fine

What Happened

In May 2023, the Irish Data Protection Commission fined Meta €1.2 billion ($1.3B) — the largest GDPR fine ever — for transferring EU user data to the United States without adequate data protection safeguards in violation of GDPR Chapter V.

Compliance Lessons

  • GDPR has teeth — even the world’s largest companies are subject to massive penalties
  • Cross-border data transfers require specific legal mechanisms
  • Compliance is not optional, regardless of company size or influence
  • The fine represented approximately 1% of Meta’s annual revenue

Other notable GDPR fines: Amazon (€746M, 2021), WhatsApp (€225M, 2021), Google (€150M, 2022). The consequences of noncompliance are very real.

Compliance Monitoring

Due Diligence

Continuously researching and understanding the legal and regulatory requirements that pertain to the organization. Stay abreast of evolving laws.

Due Care

Ongoing efforts to ensure implemented policies and controls are effective and continuously maintained. Regularly review and update policies.

Acknowledgment

Employees and partners state that they are aware of compliance requirements.

Attestation

Employees and partners confirm they are aware of requirements and that their practices adhere to those policies.

Internal Monitoring

Internal audits, reviews, and checks to ensure the organization follows its own policies.

External Monitoring

Third-party audits and assessments providing an unbiased view of compliance status.

Automation in Compliance Monitoring

Why Automate?

  • Essential for larger organizations with complex compliance requirements
  • Track changes in regulations automatically
  • Monitor for policy violations in real time
  • Ensure policies are consistently applied across the organization
  • Reduce human error
  • Generate detailed reports for analysis and improvement

Exam Tip: Be ready to summarize elements of effective security compliance, including compliance reporting, consequences of noncompliance, and compliance monitoring (due diligence, due care, attestation, acknowledgment, internal/external, automation).

Adopting Standard Frameworks

Why Use Frameworks?

Developing a cybersecurity program from scratch is a formidable undertaking. Standard security frameworks provide a roadmap and a standardized approach to developing and evaluating cybersecurity programs.

Key Frameworks for the Exam

  • NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) — broad structure for cybersecurity controls
  • NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF) — formal process for implementing controls
  • ISO 27001 — Information security management systems
  • ISO 27002 — Information security controls
  • ISO 27701 — Privacy controls
  • ISO 31000 — General risk management

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

Framework Core — Five Functions

Identify

Protect

Detect

Respond

Recover

Each function is divided into categories, subcategories, and informative references.

Three Components

  • Framework Core: Set of security functions applicable across all industries
  • Implementation Tiers: Maturity model (Tier 1: Partial → Tier 4: Adaptive)
  • Framework Profile: Describes how a specific organization approaches the Core functions

NIST CSF Implementation Tiers

Tier 1: Partial

Ad hoc, reactive risk management. Limited awareness of cybersecurity risk. No understanding of role in larger ecosystem.

Tier 2: Risk Informed

Management-approved practices, but not yet organization-wide policy. Aware of risk but no org-wide approach established.

Tier 3: Repeatable

Formally approved, organization-wide risk management. Understands role, dependencies, and dependents in the larger ecosystem.

Tier 4: Adaptive

Adapts based on lessons learned and predictive indicators. Org-wide risk-informed approach. Contributes to community’s broader understanding.

NIST CSF 2.0 (February 2024)

What Changed

NIST released CSF 2.0 in February 2024 — the first major update since the original 2014 framework. The most significant addition is a sixth core function: Govern.

The Govern Function

  • Placed at the center of the framework, connecting all other functions
  • Covers organizational context, risk management strategy, roles and responsibilities
  • Addresses policy, oversight, and cybersecurity supply chain risk management
  • Reflects growing recognition that governance is foundational to all cybersecurity activities

CSF 2.0 also expanded its scope beyond critical infrastructure to all organizations, added emphasis on supply chain risk, and improved guidance for creating Framework Profiles.

NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)

Purpose

A mandatory standard for federal agencies providing a formalized process to select, implement, and assess risk-based security and privacy controls. Defined in NIST SP 800-37.

RMF Steps

Prepare → Categorize → Select → Implement → Assess → Authorize → Monitor

CSF vs. RMF: The RMF is a formal process for implementing security controls and authorizing system use. The CSF provides a broad structure for cybersecurity controls. Both are mandatory for government; only CSF is commonly used in private industry.

ISO Standards

ISO 27001

“Information security management systems.” Control objectives covering 14 categories. Organizations can pursue formal certification by an external assessor.

ISO 27002

Goes beyond objectives to describe actual controls an organization may implement. Supplementary to 27001 for selecting and implementing controls.

ISO 27701

Standard guidance for managing privacy controls. An extension to ISO 27001/27002 focusing specifically on privacy.

ISO 31000

Guidelines for risk management programs in general. Not specific to cybersecurity or privacy — applicable to any type of risk.

Exam Tip: ISO 27001 = cybersecurity, ISO 27701 = privacy. They look nearly identical in numbering, so remember the distinction!

Benchmarks & Secure Configuration Guides

The Gap

NIST and ISO frameworks are high-level and don’t offer practical guidance on actually implementing security controls. Benchmarks and configuration guides fill this gap.

Center for Internet Security (CIS)

  • Publishes hundreds of benchmarks for commonly used platforms
  • Covers operating systems, web servers, application servers, network devices
  • Extremely detailed — the Windows Server 2022 benchmark is 642 pages
  • Specifies exact configuration settings (e.g., “Minimum password length: 14 or more characters”)

Other sources include vendor-specific hardening guides, DISA STIGs (Security Technical Implementation Guides) for government systems, and NIST National Checklist Program.

Security Awareness & Training

Why It Matters

The success of a security program depends on the behavior of many different people. Security training ensures employees understand risks and their role in minimizing them.

Training

  • Formal programs requiring time to learn material
  • Computer-based training (CBT)
  • Role-based training tailored to job responsibilities
  • Sysadmins get different training than customer service reps

Awareness

  • Less formal, designed to reinforce lessons
  • Posters, videos, email messages
  • Keeps security top-of-mind
  • No commitment of time to learn new material

Phishing & Anomalous Behavior Recognition

Anti-Phishing Campaigns

  • Every security program should include specific anti-phishing training
  • Phishing simulations — send fake phishing messages to test employees
  • Users who click are sent to additional training
  • Train users to recognize suspicious messages and report them properly

Anomalous Behavior Recognition

  • Train employees to recognize risky, unexpected, and unintentional behavior
  • The insider threat from employees with legitimate access is significant
  • Other employees may be the first to notice signs of anomalous behavior
  • Create clear channels for reporting concerns

User Guidance & Training Topics

Security Policies & Handbooks

Inform users where to find critical security documents.

Situational Awareness

Update users on current threats and how to recognize suspicious activity.

Insider Threats

Remind users that employees and insiders can pose security risks. Be alert for anomalous behavior.

Password Management

Educate on password standards and the importance of not reusing passwords.

Removable Media & Cables

Risks of USB drives, external media, and unfamiliar cables. Scan for malware.

Social Engineering

Recognize and respond to attacks. Be skeptical of unsolicited communications.

Operational Security

Protect sensitive information day-to-day. Don’t discuss sensitive data in public areas.

Hybrid/Remote Work

VPNs, secure Wi-Fi, physical device security, and remote work policies.

Training Program Lifecycle

Development

  • Assess the organization’s security landscape
  • Identify potential risks and threats
  • Develop tailored content for unique challenges
  • Incorporate real-world examples
  • Align with organizational policies and procedures

Execution

  • Use variety: workshops, e-learning, simulations
  • Make training accessible and regular
  • Initial training for new employees
  • Periodic refreshers to keep knowledge current

Reporting & Monitoring

  • Track participation in training programs
  • Assess knowledge through quizzes and tests
  • Collect feedback from employees
  • Provide reports to decision-makers
  • Analyze trends in knowledge levels and incidents

Frequency

  • Initial: When joining the organization or assuming new responsibilities
  • Recurring: Annual refresher training
  • Review and update materials regularly as the threat landscape evolves

Key Takeaways

1. Governance programs ensure organizations achieve strategic objectives through procedures and controls. Know the difference between centralized and decentralized models.

2. Policy frameworks consist of policies (mandatory, high-level), standards (mandatory, specific), procedures (mandatory, step-by-step), and guidelines (optional, advisory).

3. Change management uses a 6-step process (request, review, approve, test, implement, document) with backout plans and maintenance windows.

4. Personnel controls include least privilege, separation of duties, two-person control, job rotation, and mandatory vacations.

5. Third-party risk management covers the full vendor lifecycle: selection, assessment, agreements (SLA, MSA, MOU, MOA, NDA, BPA), monitoring, and wind-down.

6. Know key regulations (HIPAA, PCI DSS, GLBA, SOX, GDPR, FERPA), consequences of noncompliance, and frameworks (NIST CSF/RMF, ISO 27001/27002/27701/31000).
