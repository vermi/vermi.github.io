PART I

Analyzing Risk

Understanding threats, vulnerabilities, and the nature of risk

Enterprise Risk Management

What is ERM?

A formal approach to risk analysis that begins with identifying risks, continues with determining severity, and results in adopting risk management strategies to address each risk.

Why It Matters

  • Organizations face reputational, financial, and operational risks daily
  • Risk management brings order to identifying and addressing these risks
  • Cybersecurity is deeply intertwined with the protection of personal information

Key Definitions

Threats

Any possible events that might have an adverse impact on the confidentiality, integrity, and/or availability of our information or information systems.

Vulnerabilities

Weaknesses in our systems or controls that could be exploited by a threat.

Risks

Occur at the intersection of a vulnerability and a threat that might exploit it. A threat without a corresponding vulnerability does not pose a risk, nor does a vulnerability without a corresponding threat.

Threat + Vulnerability = Risk

Threat
Vulnerability
Risk

The overlap — where threat meets vulnerability

Example: A server exposes TCP port 22 to the world (vulnerability). An attacker with a brute-force tool (threat) targets it. The combination — a successful SSH brute-force attack — is the risk. Close the port and the risk disappears.

Risk Identification

The risk identification process requires identifying the threats and vulnerabilities that exist in your operating environment. These may come from a wide variety of sources.

External Risks

Cybersecurity adversaries, malicious code, natural disasters, and other risks originating outside the organization.

Internal Risks

Malicious insiders, mistakes by authorized users, equipment failures, and similar risks from within.

Multiparty Risks

Risks that impact more than one organization (e.g., power outage on a city block, compromise of a SaaS provider).

Legacy Systems

Outdated systems that no longer receive security updates, requiring extraordinary protective measures.

More Risk Categories

Intellectual Property (IP) Theft

Risk when a company possesses trade secrets or proprietary information that, if disclosed, could compromise its business advantage.

Software Compliance / Licensing

Risk when an organization licenses software and violates usage limitations, exposing the organization to financial and legal risk.

Remember: Risk categories include financial risk, reputational risk, strategic risk, operational risk, and compliance risk. All should be considered during identification.

Risk Assessment

Two Key Factors

  • Likelihood of occurrence (probability) — the percent chance a threat will exploit a vulnerability over a given period
  • Impact (magnitude) — the damage the risk will cause if it occurs (financial cost, reputation, etc.)

Risk Severity = Likelihood × Impact

Think of this as a conceptual formula — combining probability and magnitude to determine severity.

Example: Hit by a bicycle — moderate impact, high probability = worth mitigating. Hit by a meteor — catastrophic impact, near-zero probability = accept the risk.

Types of Risk Assessment

One-Time

A point-in-time snapshot. May be done in response to a security incident or at management’s request.

Ad Hoc

Conducted in response to a specific event — a new project, technology implementation, or significant business change.

Recurring

Performed at regular intervals (annually, quarterly) to track the evolution of risks and monitor the risk profile over time.

Continuous

Ongoing monitoring and analysis, often using automated systems that constantly scan for new threats and changes.

Exam Tip: These four types are specifically mentioned in the CompTIA exam objectives. Be prepared to explain any one of them.

PART II

Risk Analysis

Quantitative and qualitative approaches to evaluating risk

Risk Analysis

Risk analysis is a formalized approach to risk prioritization. It allows organizations to conduct reviews in a structured manner. There are two methodologies:

Quantitative

Uses numeric data for straightforward prioritization. Expresses risk in dollar values.

Qualitative

Uses subjective judgments and categories (Low/Medium/High) for risks that are difficult to quantify.

Many organizations combine both techniques to get a well-rounded picture of tangible and intangible risks.

Quantitative Risk Analysis

1

Determine the Asset Value (AV)

The value of the asset in dollars — cost to acquire, replace, or the depreciated value.

2

Determine the Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)

How many times per year you expect the risk to occur. Twice a year = 2.0; once every 100 years = 0.01.

3

Determine the Exposure Factor (EF)

Percentage of the asset expected to be damaged. Total destruction = 100%; half damaged = 50%.

4

Calculate the Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)

SLE = AV × EF — the financial damage expected each time the risk materializes.

5

Calculate the Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE)

ALE = SLE × ARO — the total expected damage from this risk per year.

The Formulas You Must Know

SLE = AV × EF

Single Loss Expectancy = Asset Value × Exposure Factor

ALE = SLE × ARO

Annualized Loss Expectancy = Single Loss Expectancy × Annualized Rate of Occurrence

Exam Tip: Be prepared to explain and calculate these values. Watch out for scenarios that provide extra information to lead you astray!

Worked Example: DoS Attack

Your email server generates $1,000/hour in sales. A DoS attack is expected 3 times per year, lasting 3 hours each time. During the attack the server operates at 10% capacity.

Asset Value (AV)

$1,000/hr × 3 hrs = $3,000

Exposure Factor (EF)

90% capacity lost = 0.90

SLE = AV × EF

$3,000 × 0.90 = $2,700

ARO

3 times per year = 3.0

ALE = SLE × ARO

$2,700 × 3.0 = $8,100 per year

A DoS prevention service costing less than $8,100/year makes financial sense.

Qualitative Risk Analysis

For risks that are hard to quantify in dollar terms — like reputational damage, employee morale, or public health — qualitative techniques use subjective rating scales (Low/Medium/High) to evaluate probability and magnitude.

Supply Chain Assessment

Vendor Due Diligence

You rely on many vendors to protect the CIA of your data. Cloud providers, SaaS platforms, and hardware suppliers are all part of your supply chain. Performing vendor due diligence is a crucial security responsibility.

Hardware Source Authenticity

Verify that hardware was not tampered with after leaving the vendor. Leaked NSA documents revealed the U.S. government intercepted hardware shipments to implant malicious code.

Key takeaway: Your organization’s risk extends beyond your own walls. Assess vendors and supply chains as part of your overall risk management program.

PART III

Managing & Tracking Risk

Strategies, tracking tools, and business impact analysis

Risk Management Strategies

Once risks are analyzed, managers select an appropriate strategy for each risk. There are four primary strategies:

Mitigation

Apply security controls to reduce probability and/or magnitude.

Avoidance

Change business practices to completely eliminate the risk.

Transference

Shift the impact to another entity (e.g., insurance).

Acceptance

Deliberately choose to take no action and accept the risk.

Risk Mitigation

Definition

Applying security controls to reduce the probability and/or magnitude of a risk. This is the most common strategy — the vast majority of security work revolves around mitigation.

Laptop Theft Example

  • Cable locks → reduce probability of theft
  • Tamperproof STOP tags → deter thieves (reduce probability) and aid recovery (reduce impact)

DDoS Example

  • More bandwidth / server capacity → reduce impact
  • Third-party DDoS mitigation service → reduce probability of traffic reaching your network

Risk Avoidance

Definition

Change business practices to completely eliminate the potential that a risk will materialize. The major drawback: avoidance strategies typically have a serious detrimental impact on the business.

Laptop Theft

Don’t allow employees to use laptops. Eliminates the risk, but severely harms productivity.

DDoS Attack

Shut down the website. No website = no DDoS risk. The “ultimate denial-of-service attack!”

Avoidance is rarely practical — it is the most extreme strategy and is typically used only when the risk far outweighs the benefit of the activity.

Risk Transference

Definition

Shift some of the impact of a risk to another entity. The most common example: purchasing an insurance policy that covers the risk.

Examples

  • Laptop theft: Property insurance reimburses the value of the stolen device
  • DDoS attack: Cybersecurity insurance covers the cost of recovering operations and lost revenue

Note: Many general business insurance policies exclude cybersecurity risks. Organizations should purchase specific cybersecurity insurance for cyber threats.

Risk Acceptance

Definition

Deliberately choosing to take no other risk management strategy and continue operations as normal. Warranted when the cost of mitigation exceeds the impact of the risk itself.

Exceptions & Exemptions

  • Exception: A risk doesn’t align with policy but the cost of mitigation is too high — granted for that specific case
  • Exemption: More formal than an exception; may require higher-level approval and documented decision-making; may have an expiration date

Warning: Risk acceptance is a deliberate decision from thoughtful analysis. Simply stating “we accept this risk” without analysis is not acceptance — it is an unmanaged risk!

Risk Tracking Concepts

Inherent Risk

The original level of risk before implementing any controls. Inherent in the organization’s business.

Residual Risk

The risk that remains after implementing controls to mitigate, avoid, and/or transfer the inherent risk.

Goal: An organization implements risk management strategies to reduce inherent risk. It continues until the residual risk is at or below the organization’s risk appetite.

Appetite, Tolerance & Threshold

Risk Appetite

The broad level of risk an organization is willing to accept as a cost of doing business.

Risk Threshold

The specific quantitative boundary at which a risk becomes unacceptable and triggers action. More precise than appetite.

Risk Tolerance

The organization’s ability to withstand risks and continue operations without significant impact.

Types of Risk Appetite

Expansionary

Willing to take on higher levels of risk in pursuit of higher rewards. Suitable for organizations looking to aggressively grow, innovate, or capture market share.

Neutral

A balanced approach — willing to take moderate risk for steady growth. Aims for stability and more secure investments.

Conservative

Tends to avoid high risks and focuses on maintaining stability and protecting existing assets. Common in highly regulated industries.

KRIs & Risk Owners

Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)

Metrics used to measure and provide early warning signals for increasing levels of risk. They help track the effectiveness of risk mitigation efforts and ensure residual risk stays within appetite.

Risk Owner

An individual or entity responsible for managing and monitoring risks, including implementing necessary controls and actions to mitigate them.

Exam Tip: Risk owner, risk threshold, and KRIs are the three risk register elements specifically mentioned in the CompTIA exam objectives.

Risk Register

What Is It?

The primary tool that risk management professionals use to track risks facing the organization. It documents each risk along with its assessment, owner, and status.

Common Data Elements

  • Risk statement, causes, and impacts
  • Risk owner
  • Risk threshold information
  • Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)
  • Likelihood, impact, and overall score

Risk Matrix / Heat Map

A summarized view used to communicate risk posture to senior leaders, plotting risks by likelihood vs. impact using color-coded cells (green, yellow, red).

Risk Reporting

Communicating the status and evolution of risks to stakeholders. Tailor format to the audience — dashboards for executives, detailed data for risk teams.

Regular Updates

Routine reports on risk status, control effectiveness, and recent changes.

Dashboard Reporting

Visual aids (graphs, charts) summarizing risk data in real time.

Ad Hoc Reports

Produced as needed in response to specific events requiring immediate attention.

Risk Trend Analysis

Analyzing historical data to identify patterns and predict future risks.

Disaster Recovery Planning

What is DRP?

The discipline of developing plans to recover operations as quickly as possible in the face of a disaster. A disaster is any event that has the potential to disrupt an organization’s business.

Disaster Types

  • Natural: Hurricanes, floods, earthquakes, tornadoes
  • Human-made: Cyberattacks, infrastructure failures, human error
  • Internal & External: Both forces within and outside the organization

Organizations should conduct site risk assessments for each facility, identifying and prioritizing risks from both environmental and human-made sources.

Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

A formal process designed to identify the mission-essential functions within an organization and the critical systems that support them.

MTBF

Mean Time Between Failures — the expected time that will elapse between system failures. MTBF of 6 months = expect a failure every 6 months.

MTTR

Mean Time to Repair — the average amount of time to restore a system to normal operating state after a failure.

RTO

Recovery Time Objective — the amount of time an organization can tolerate a system being down. Service team meets expectations when MTTR < RTO.

RPO

Recovery Point Objective — the amount of data an organization can tolerate losing during an outage. Drives backup frequency.

Single Points of Failure

Definition

Systems, devices, or other components that, if they fail, would cause an outage. Identifying and eliminating single points of failure is critical to disaster recovery.

Example: Power Supply

A server with one power supply — if it fails, the server goes down. Adding a redundant power supply resolves this.

Example: Web Server

A single web server serving your site — if it fails, the site is down. Adding a second server to a cluster eliminates the SPOF.

PART IV

Privacy

Protecting personal information, data classification, roles, and privacy-enhancing technologies

Privacy

Why Privacy Matters

Cybersecurity professionals protect the CIA of all information, including personally identifiable information (PII). Breaches expose individuals to identity theft and expose organizations to reputational damage and fines.

Legal Implications

Privacy requirements vary by jurisdiction — local, regional, national, and global. Organizations must understand the legal implications of where they operate.

Privacy Notice

A document outlining the organization’s privacy commitments. May be required by law or regulation, and may be included in terms of agreement.

Data Inventory

The first step in managing sensitive data: develop a data inventory of information types maintained and where that data is stored, processed, and transmitted.

PII

Personally Identifiable Information — uniquely identifies a person (customers, employees, third parties).

PHI

Protected Health Information — medical records subject to HIPAA.

Financial Information

Personal financial records subject to GLBA and/or PCI DSS.

Intellectual Property

Trade secrets, formulas, manufacturing processes, proprietary business information.

Legal Information

Attorney-client privilege, contracts, court records, regulatory filings.

Regulated Information

Any data governed by laws or regulations (HIPAA, GLBA, PCI DSS, etc.).

Exam Tip: The data inventory must include all forms of information — both human-readable and non-human-readable (e.g., binary format data files containing PII).

Information Classification

Classification programs organize data into categories based on sensitivity and the impact of unauthorized disclosure.

Top Secret

Highest degree of protection. Unauthorized disclosure could cause exceptionally grave damage to national security.

Secret

Substantial protection. Disclosure could cause serious damage to national security.

Confidential

Some protection. Disclosure could cause identifiable damage to national security.

Unclassified

Does not meet classification standards, but is still not publicly releasable without authorization.

Business Classification Levels

Businesses use friendlier terms than the government. There are no “official” business classification levels — each organization may define them differently.

Data Roles & Responsibilities

Data Owners

Senior executives designated as responsible for different data types. Example: VP of HR owns employment data; VP of Sales owns customer data.

Data Subjects

Individuals whose personal data is being processed (customers, employees, partners). They often have rights to access, correct, or request deletion.

Data Controllers

Entities that determine the reasons and methods for processing personal information. A European law term substituting for “data owner.”

More Data Roles

Data Stewards

Carry out the intent of the data controller. Delegated responsibility from the controller for day-to-day data management.

Data Custodians

Responsible for the secure safekeeping of information, but do not have controller or stewardship responsibility. Example: the IT security team securing PII on behalf of a data controller.

Data Processors

Service providers that process personal information on behalf of a data controller. Example: a credit card processing company acting as a processor for a retailer.

GDPR Note: The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation requires every data controller to designate a Data Protection Officer (DPO) who bears ultimate responsibility for the organization’s data privacy efforts.

Controller vs. Processor

Data Controller

  • Determines why data is processed
  • Determines how data is processed
  • Retains overall responsibility
  • Decides what data to collect
  • Example: A retailer collecting customer info

Data Processor

  • Processes data on behalf of the controller
  • Follows the controller’s instructions
  • Does not determine purpose of processing
  • Must protect data per agreement
  • Example: A credit card processing service

Information Life Cycle

Data protection should continue at all stages of the information life cycle, from collection to eventual disposal.

Data Minimization

Collect only the smallest possible amount of information necessary. Information that is not necessary should be discarded or not collected in the first place.

Purpose Limitation

Information should be used only for the purpose for which it was originally collected and consented to by the data subjects.

Data Retention

Standards that guide the end of the data life cycle. Data should be kept only as long as necessary, then securely destroyed.

Exam Tip: Reducing the amount of data you retain is one of the most effective strategies for reducing security risk.

Right to Be Forgotten

What Is It?

Also known as the right to erasure — a concept implemented in data protection laws (notably the EU’s GDPR) that allows individuals to request the deletion of their personal data.

When Can Individuals Request Erasure?

  • Data is no longer needed for its original purpose
  • The individual withdraws consent
  • The individual objects and there is no overriding legitimate interest
  • The data has been unlawfully processed
  • There is a legal obligation to erase the data

Implementing this right is challenging — organizations need proper mechanisms to identify and erase data across all systems when requested.

Privacy Enhancing Technologies

When data can’t be completely removed, transform it to anonymize or obscure the original information. The deidentification process removes the ability to link data back to an individual.

Hashing

Apply a hash function to transform a value into a hash value, replacing the original in the dataset. Caution: Vulnerable to rainbow table attacks if the set of possible values is small.

Tokenization

Replace sensitive values with a unique identifier using a lookup table. Example: replace a student ID with a random 10-digit number. Keep the lookup table secure!

Data Masking

Partially redact sensitive information by replacing characters with blanks, Xs, or *s. Example: credit card ****-****-****-1234.

Rainbow Table Attack

The Problem with Hashing Alone

If an attacker has a list of possible values for a field, they can compute the hash of each candidate value and check whether those hashes appear in your data file.

Example

A college hashes student IDs in a list of students who failed courses. An attacker with the full student roster can hash every ID and check which hashes match — revealing who failed.

Lesson: Hashing should be used with caution as a deidentification technique. It works best when the set of possible values is very large and unpredictable.

Data Breach Notification

Incident Response

In the event of a data breach, immediately activate the cybersecurity incident response plan. Notify key personnel and escalate serious incidents.

Notification Requirements

  • May need to notify affected individuals
  • May need to notify government regulators and/or news media
  • In the U.S., every state has a data breach notification law with different requirements
  • The EU’s GDPR includes a breach notification requirement

Key Takeaway

Requirements vary by industry and jurisdiction. Organizations should consult with an attorney well-versed in this field when experiencing a breach.

ACTIVITY

Risk Calculation Practice

Work through these scenarios using quantitative risk analysis

Scenario 1: A web server worth $50,000 faces a risk that would destroy 60% of its value. The risk is expected to occur once every 5 years. Calculate the SLE, ARO, and ALE.

Scenario 2: A database containing customer records has an AV of $200,000. A breach (EF=25%) is expected twice per year. What is the ALE? Would a $60,000/year security tool be worth it?

Activity Answers

Scenario 1

  • AV = $50,000
  • EF = 60% (0.60)
  • SLE = $50,000 × 0.60 = $30,000
  • ARO = 1/5 = 0.2
  • ALE = $30,000 × 0.2 = $6,000/year

Scenario 2

  • AV = $200,000
  • EF = 25% (0.25)
  • SLE = $200,000 × 0.25 = $50,000
  • ARO = 2.0
  • ALE = $50,000 × 2.0 = $100,000/year
  • $60,000 tool < $100,000 ALE → Yes, worth it

ACTIVITY

Identify the Strategy

For each scenario, identify which risk management strategy is being used

A: A company purchases cyber insurance to cover losses from ransomware attacks.

B: A hospital decides not to offer public Wi-Fi to patients to prevent network-based attacks.

C: An organization installs endpoint detection and response (EDR) software on all workstations.

D: A startup acknowledges the risk of a small data leak but decides the cost of encryption is not justified for non-sensitive marketing data.

Activity Answers

A: Risk Transference

Purchasing insurance shifts the financial impact to the insurance carrier.

B: Risk Avoidance

Eliminating public Wi-Fi completely removes the risk of network-based attacks through that vector.

C: Risk Mitigation

EDR software reduces the probability and impact of malware infections — a security control applied to reduce risk.

D: Risk Acceptance

A deliberate, informed decision that the cost of mitigation outweighs the impact of the risk.

Exam Essentials

Risk Identification & Assessment

Cybersecurity analysts identify all risks facing the organization, then conduct a business impact analysis to assess the degree of risk based on probability and magnitude.

Vendors Are a Source of External Risk

Conduct supply chain assessments and vendor due diligence. Perform hardware source authenticity checks to verify equipment was not tampered with.

Four Risk Management Strategies

Mitigation (reduce probability/impact), Avoidance (eliminate the risk), Transference (shift impact to third party), Acceptance (acknowledge and proceed). For acceptance: know the difference between exceptions and exemptions.

Exam Essentials (cont.)

Disaster Recovery Planning Builds Resiliency

DRPs activate when a natural or human-made disaster disrupts operations. Know the BIA metrics: MTBF, MTTR, RTO, RPO.

Privacy Controls Protect Personal Information

Programs should cover PII, PHI, financial information, and other records. Understand data minimization, purpose limitation, data retention, and the right to be forgotten.
