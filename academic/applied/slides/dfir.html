The Incident Response Process

Building the foundation for responding to security incidents

Events vs. Incidents

Event

An observable occurrence in a system or network. Events happen constantly — most are routine.

Example: A user logs in, a firewall blocks a port scan, a scheduled backup runs.

Incident

A violation of the organization's policies and procedures or security practices.

Example: Unauthorized access, malware infection, data breach, insider threat.

Many events occur → Few are incidents. The challenge is detecting which events are actually incidents. Some organizations use the term security incident to distinguish from ITSM incidents.

Phase 1: Preparation

The foundation of incident response. Everything you do before an incident determines how well you handle it.

Build the Team

  • Identify who will be on the IR team
  • Define roles, responsibilities, and leadership
  • Ensure proper training for all members

Build the Plans

  • Create and document IR procedures
  • Define communication protocols
  • Develop runbooks and playbooks

Build the Infrastructure

  • Acquire and configure security tools
  • Deploy monitoring and logging
  • Establish forensic capabilities

Test the Plans

  • Conduct exercises regularly
  • Validate tools and processes work
  • Update plans based on results

Phases 2 & 3: Detection & Analysis

2. Detection

  • Review events to identify potential incidents
  • Leverage indicators of compromise (IoCs) and security monitoring
  • Use log analysis and SIEM correlation capabilities
  • Requires a comprehensive awareness and reporting program for staff

3. Analysis

  • Determine scope: what was targeted, what is the impact?
  • Identify related events and affected systems
  • Correlate data from multiple sources to build a complete picture
  • Determine if the event should be escalated to a full incident

Detection and Analysis often overlap. You may detect something, analyze it, detect more, and re-analyze — it's iterative.

Phases 4-6: Containment, Eradication & Recovery

4. Containment

  • Prevent further damage — quarantine, isolate, or segment affected systems
  • May not be complete if elements are missed during analysis
  • Balance: stop the threat vs. preserve forensic evidence

5. Eradication

  • Remove all artifacts of the incident from affected systems
  • Often involves rebuilding or restoring systems from clean backups
  • Proving a system is "fully clean" can be very difficult

6. Recovery

  • Restore systems and services to normal operations
  • Implement fixes so the same vulnerability cannot be re-exploited
  • Requires eradication to be successful first

Lessons Learned

After recovery, organizations conduct a lessons learned session to ensure they improve and don't make the same mistakes again. This feeds directly back into the Preparation phase.

What to Document

  • What happened and when?
  • What worked well in the response?
  • What failed or was missing?
  • What could be done differently next time?

Outcomes

  • Updated IR plans and procedures
  • New or revised security controls
  • Additional training needs identified
  • Patching, permission, or architecture changes

Building the IR Team

Management / Leadership

Makes decisions and serves as conduit to senior leadership. Needs enough seniority to act during emergencies.

Information Security

Core of the team. Brings specialized IR and analysis skills. Handles containment using security tools.

Technical Experts

Sysadmins, developers, architects. System familiarity helps find artifacts others might miss.

Communications / PR

Manages internal and external messaging. Poor communications can make incidents far worse.

Legal / HR

Legal counsel for compliance and contracts. HR if insider threats or personnel issues are involved.

Law Enforcement

Added when specific attacks or legal issues require their involvement. Not typically part of every incident.

IR Exercises

Tabletop Exercises

  • Discussion-based — "walk through" a scenario
  • Team members talk through their roles and responses
  • Resembles a brainstorming session
  • Low cost, identifies gaps in plans and procedures
  • No actual systems are affected

Simulations

  • Active testing — simulate actual incidents
  • Can be partial (one function) or full-scale
  • All participants must know it's an exercise
  • Start every call/email with "This is an exercise"
  • Scoped carefully to avoid impacting production

Keeping exercises properly under control is more important than detailed testing. Use scripts for specific performance requirements.

Incident Response Plans

IR plans can include several subplans. They need to be regularly reviewed and tested — an outdated plan can be as bad as no plan at all.

Communication Plan

Who communicates, to whom, through what channels. Defines who talks to media and makes the final call on tone and content.

Stakeholder Management Plan

Prioritize which stakeholders get updates, what support they need, and how they interact with the IR process.

Business Continuity (BC) Plan

Keep the organization functional during incidents. May involve alternate systems or offloading services.

Disaster Recovery (DR) Plan

Restore operations after major disasters (natural or human-made) that destroy facilities or infrastructure.

Policies & Training

IR Policies

  • Policy: High-level statement of management intent — slow to change
  • Standard: Specific guidance on what should be done — points to a policy
  • Procedure: Step-by-step instructions for how to do it — updated frequently
  • IR policies identify the team, define authority, and require creation of handling procedures

Training

  • Regular training for all IR team members
  • IR certifications for security professionals
  • CISA offers free IR training covering attack prevention, IoCs, and log management
  • Practice as a group to ensure the whole team can work together

An IR policy is not the only policy your organization needs. Many IT policies (acceptable use, access control, change management) impact incident response.

Threat Hunting & Security Monitoring

Detecting the bad, understanding the tools

Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)

Threat hunters look for indicators of compromise — data points commonly associated with malicious actors and incidents.

Account Lockout

Brute-force attempts causing repeated failed logins

Concurrent Session Usage

Same user logged in from multiple locations simultaneously

Impossible Travel

Login from New York, then London 10 minutes later

Blocked Content

Repeated attempts to access blocked domains or resources — may indicate malware or intentional access attempts

Indicators of Compromise (cont.)

Resource Consumption

Unusual disk, bandwidth, or CPU usage spikes. Requires context — only concerning if much higher than usual.

Resource Inaccessibility

Systems, files, or services suddenly unavailable. Investigate to rule out or confirm malicious cause.

Out-of-Cycle Logging

Activity at unexpected times: a 2 a.m. login from a 9-to-5 employee, or a job running off schedule.

Missing Logs

Attacker may have wiped logs to cover tracks. This is why centralized logging is critical.

Published/Documented IoCs are discovered, documented, and shared via threat feeds and information sharing organizations (ISACs). These allow organizations to proactively hunt for known threats.

Attack Frameworks

Incident responders use frameworks to describe attacks using common language and terminology.

MITRE ATT&CK

Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge. The most comprehensive and widely used framework. Covers the full threat lifecycle from reconnaissance through impact. Supported in most security tools.

Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain

Models attack phases linearly: Reconnaissance → Weaponization → Delivery → Exploitation → Installation → Command & Control → Actions on Objectives.

Diamond Model

Analyzes intrusions by relating four core features: Adversary, Capability, Infrastructure, and Victim. Useful for threat intelligence analysis.

MITRE ATT&CK: Deeper Look

Tactics

The "why" — the adversary's goal at each stage

Techniques

The "how" — methods used to achieve the goal

Procedures

Specific implementations by real threat actors

Matrices Available

  • Enterprise: Windows, macOS, Linux, Cloud Computing
  • Mobile: iOS and Android
  • Includes data sources, threat actor groups, software, and detailed technique definitions

Each Technique Entry Includes:

ID number, tactic classification, platforms it applies to, permissions required, relevant data sources, detection guidance, and mitigations.

Monitoring Computing Resources

Systems Monitoring

  • System logs and central management tools (including cloud)
  • System health and performance data aggregated at central logging servers

Application Monitoring

  • Application logs, management interfaces, performance monitoring
  • Varies significantly — each app must be analyzed for monitoring needs

Infrastructure Monitoring

  • Routers, switches, firewalls generate logs via SNMP and syslog
  • Different devices produce different log levels and formats
  • Hardware vendors often provide their own management tools

SIEM: Security Information & Event Management

The central security monitoring tool. Collects, correlates, and analyzes log data from across the entire organization.

What SIEM Does

  • Collects and aggregates log data from all sources
  • Correlates events using rules and heuristics
  • Provides dashboards and visual reporting
  • Generates alerts based on rules and thresholds
  • Supports compliance requirements with built-in modules
  • May include ML/AI for user behavior analytics

Data Inputs

  • System and application logs
  • Network security device logs
  • Network infrastructure logs
  • Packet captures
  • Cloud service integrations (Google, Office 365, Okta, etc.)

You may also see the terms SIM (Security Information Management) and SEM (Security Event Management). SIEM is the converged term.

SIEM Components

Sensors

  • Software agents (or VMs/dedicated devices) deployed to gather data
  • Placed in cloud, remote datacenters, or anywhere data needs collecting
  • May preprocess data before forwarding to the SIEM
  • Must be secured like any other network component

Dashboards

  • High-level visual representation of security state
  • Configurable per analyst or per organizational need
  • Help quickly identify problems, abnormal patterns, and trends
  • Multiple dashboards for different views

Rules & Correlation Engine

  • Rules use logic to determine when alerts should fire
  • Can trigger actions: alerts, firewall changes, defenses
  • Poorly written rules cause false positives or miss real threats
  • Must be carefully built, tested, and regularly reviewed

Sensitivity & Thresholds

  • Control the volume of alerts the SIEM generates
  • Alert after N occurrences, or only for high-value targets
  • Balance: catch threats vs. avoid noise

Alert Tuning & Alert Fatigue

Alert Tuning

  • Modify alerts to fire only on events that matter
  • Set thresholds and filter rules
  • Identify and suppress false alarms and normal behaviors
  • Ensure tuning is not so aggressive that real threats are missed

Alert Fatigue

  • One of the biggest threats to SIEM deployments
  • Too many alerts → analysts stop responding
  • Caused by non-critical, high-volume, or false-positive alerts
  • When a real event occurs, it may be missed or ignored
  • Results in much worse security incidents

Trend Analysis

SIEM dashboards can show trends over time: new malware families emerging, exploit attempts increasing, or shifts in attacker behavior. Trends help organizations identify threats as they rise.

Log Aggregation, Correlation & Analysis

Log Aggregation

  • Collecting logs from many sources into one location
  • Not just SIEM — also syslog-ng, rsyslog, and similar tools
  • Lines blur between SIEM, SOAR, and other analysis tools

Correlation

  • Matching individual data points to other data points across sources
  • Requires: time stamps, system identifiers, user accounts, and other details
  • Automated correlation matches known events and IoCs to build a complete dataset
  • Enables tagging, investigation tracking, and evidence building

Individual data points have limited value. Correlation is what turns raw logs into actionable intelligence.

SOAR: Security Orchestration, Automation & Response

SOAR platforms integrate with SIEM and other tools to automate repetitive security tasks, orchestrate workflows across tools, and respond to incidents faster.

Orchestration

Connect and coordinate multiple security tools and data sources

Automation

Run playbooks automatically for common incident types

Response

Quickly assess attack surface, remediate, and restore

Logs, Mitigation & Recovery

The data that tells the story and the techniques that stop the bleeding

Log Types for Incident Response

Firewall Logs

Blocked/allowed traffic. NGFW and UTM devices add application-layer details and IDS/IPS data.

Application Logs

Web server requests (Apache, IIS), errors, installer activity. Can reveal SQLi and XSS attacks via logged requests.

Endpoint Logs

Installation, system, and service logs from workstations and servers.

OS-Specific Security Logs

Windows Security log for login events. Linux: /var/log/auth.log (Debian) or /var/log/secure (Red Hat).

IDS/IPS Logs

Attack signatures detected (IDS) or blocked (IPS) in network traffic.

Network Logs

Router/switch logs, configuration changes, traffic information, network flows, and packet capture data.

Always verify log integrity — attackers target logs. Centralized logging protects against local log tampering.

Windows & Linux Log Locations

Windows Event Viewer

  • Application log: App installs, errors, license checks
  • Security log: Failed/successful logins, audit events
  • System log: OS-level events, driver issues
  • Can be exported for SIEM ingestion

Linux Log Locations

  • /var/log/ — starting point for most logs
  • /var/log/syslog — Debian/Ubuntu
  • /var/log/messages — Red Hat
  • /var/log/auth.log — authentication
  • Use grep, tail, or journalctl to search

journalctl (systemd)

Query the systemd journal for kernel, services, and boot messages.

journalctl -b                  # entries since last boot

journalctl --since "2026-04-13 08:00:00"  # filter by time

Logging Protocols & Tools

syslog

Traditional Linux logging protocol. Clients send messages to a central syslog server.

rsyslog

"Rocket-fast" syslog. High message rates, TLS encryption, TCP transport, multiple database backends.

syslog-ng

Enhanced filtering, direct database logging, TLS over TCP. Popular rsyslog alternative.

NXLog

Open source log aggregation. Parses many formats and forwards to SIEM and analysis tools.

Agents vs. Agentless Collection

Agent-based

Software installed on endpoints to collect and forward logs. More control and data.

Agentless

Uses standard interfaces like syslog. Simpler deployment, less overhead.

Network Flows

Network flows track traffic metadata: source, destination, volume, and timing. Think of them like phone records — you know who called whom and for how long, but not what was said.

NetFlow

Cisco proprietary, software-driven

sFlow

Multi-vendor, sampling-based

IPFIX

Open standard based on NetFlow v9

Strengths

Identify what traffic was sent, where it went, and where it came from. Great for spotting data exfiltration and lateral movement.

Limitations

Flows are often sampled (10:1 or 1000:1), so some detail is lost. No packet content — just metadata.

Metadata as a Data Source

Metadata is data about other data. It's generated as a normal part of operations and can be invaluable for incident response and forensics.

Email Metadata

Sender, recipient, timestamps, routing headers, attachment presence, antispam info.

Mobile Metadata

Call logs, SMS, GPS location, cellular tower data, data usage records.

Web Metadata

Metatags, cookies, headers, tracking data embedded in websites.

File Metadata (EXIF)

Creation date, author, GPS coordinates, camera model, firmware version. Powerful for forensics.

Benchmarks, Reporting & Archiving

Benchmarks

  • Define standard security configurations including logging settings
  • May require central logging, specific alert levels, and logging of critical events
  • Ensure all systems are configured to log useful information at scale

Reporting & Archiving

  • Reporting: Identify trends, provide visibility, support management oversight
  • Archiving: Retain logs that are no longer in active use for compliance and future reference
  • Common retention periods: 30, 45, 90, or 180 days (some cases: 1+ year)
  • Retention has costs: storage hardware and potential legal disclosure obligations

Containment & Mitigation Techniques

Application Allow Lists

Only approved applications can run. Everything else is blocked from installing or executing.

Application Deny Lists

Block known-bad applications. Less restrictive than allow lists — anything not on the list can still run.

Quarantine

Place suspicious files in a safe zone for investigation rather than deleting. Preserves evidence and allows recovery if it's a false positive.

Monitoring

Validate that remediation was successful. Watch for attacker re-entry or continued compromise after containment.

Isolation, Containment & Segmentation

Isolation

Move the system to a protected space. Remove from the network entirely or move to an isolation VLAN. The system is completely separated from other systems.

Containment

Leave the system in place but restrict what it can do via firewall rules or similar controls. Preserves forensic state but limits the attacker. Tension between rapid response and forensic preservation.

Segmentation

Separate systems into zones using security, network, or physical boundaries. Move infected systems away from critical assets, or move critical assets to a more protected segment.

Exam tip: Know the difference between these three. Isolation = remove, Containment = restrict in place, Segmentation = separate into zones.

Common Remediation Actions

Configuration changes are one of the most frequently used tools in containment and remediation. They must be tracked and recorded — changes may need to be rolled back after the incident.

Firewall Rule Changes

Add, modify, or remove rules to block attack traffic

MDM Policy Changes

Remote wipe, lock devices, update mobile policies, locate devices

DLP Configuration

Block new data types from leaving the organization during the incident

Content / URL Filtering

Block C2 domains, prevent phishing responses, stop malware callbacks

Certificate Revocation

Revoke compromised certificates, remove untrusted CAs from trust lists

Consider Operational Impact

Changes may break business functions. Involve stakeholders when urgency allows.

Root Cause Analysis (RCA)

After mitigation, identify why the incident occurred and fix the systemic issues. RCA feeds directly into the Lessons Learned and Preparation phases.

Five Whys

Ask "why?" repeatedly to drill to the underlying cause

Why infected? → Unpatched → Why? → No patch process → ...

Event Analysis

Examine each event: is it a root cause or a consequence of something else?

Diagramming

Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams map causes and effects visually

RCA is not about assigning blame. It's about preventing the same class of incident from happening again.

Digital Forensics

Acquiring, preserving, and analyzing digital evidence

Digital Forensic Concepts

What is Digital Forensics?

  • Acquisition and analysis of digital data — drives, files, RAM, network traffic
  • Used for legal cases, internal investigations, incident response, and intelligence
  • Requires careful documentation, timelines, and evidence handling
  • The human side matters too — interviews can provide important clues

Key Principles

  • Evidence must be intact, unaltered, and provably so
  • Analysts must demonstrate appropriate skills and use accepted tools
  • All actions must be documented in an auditable trail
  • Findings must be repeatable by a third party

Legal Holds

A legal hold (litigation hold) is a notice requiring an organization to preserve data and records relevant to pending or anticipated litigation. It cannot be ignored.

What Must Be Preserved

  • Backups and electronic files of all kinds
  • Paper documents
  • Any data that might be destroyed or modified in normal operations

Spoliation of Evidence

  • Intentionally or negligently altering, destroying, fabricating, hiding, or withholding evidence
  • Severe legal consequences — can be a devastating blow in court
  • Having a strong legal hold process before a hold arrives is critical

E-Discovery (EDRM)

The Electronic Discovery Reference Model describes 9 stages of the discovery process, used for legal cases, FOIA requests, and investigations.

1

Assess what data exists

2

Identification

Locate stored information

3

Ensure data isn't changed

4

Gather the information

5

Processing

Remove irrelevant data

6

Review

Verify completeness

7

Analysis

Identify key elements

8

Provide to third parties

9

Present in court/analysis

Order of Volatility

Capture the most volatile data first — it disappears fastest.

1

CPU Cache & Registers

Most volatile — changes constantly, rarely captured directly

2

Routing Table, ARP Cache, Process Table, Kernel Stats

Ephemeral system data — snapshot in time only

3

System Memory (RAM)

Encryption keys, running processes, ephemeral app data

4

Temporary Files & Swap Space

Lost on reboot/shutdown

5

Data on Hard Disk

Persistent but can be overwritten — full disk image required

6

Remote Logs

Centralized logs — less volatile

7

Backups

Least volatile — archived and retained

Where to Find Forensic Data

RAM / Memory

Encryption keys, ephemeral data, running processes. Very helpful for live investigations.

Swap / Pagefile

Supplements RAM. Contains fragments of running processes. Changes faster than disk files.

Disk — Full Image

Capture entire disk (not just files) to preserve deleted files and metadata. Primary focus of many investigations.

Windows Registry

Common analysis target. Many activities modify the Registry, leaving forensic traces.

Firmware

Less common target, but may be relevant if firmware was modified during the incident.

Mobile / IoT Devices

Smartphones, tablets, embedded systems may contain forensically relevant data.

VM Snapshots

Capture VM state without imaging the entire host. Can be imported into forensic tools.

Network Traffic & Logs

Packet captures, firewall logs, IDS/IPS logs, email server logs, authentication logs.

Chain of Custody

What It Documents

  • Every person who handled the evidence
  • When, where, and what was done each time
  • Sign-off forms for every access, transfer, or copy
  • Applied even when no legal case is anticipated

Evidence Admissibility

  • Must be relevant and reliable
  • Obtained legally
  • Proven authentic and unaltered
  • Best evidence available
  • Process must withstand court challenges

Admissibility requires that forensic data be intact and provably unaltered before and during the forensic process.

Forensic Copies vs. Logical Copies

Forensic Copy (Bit-for-Bit)

  • Exact duplicate of the entire drive
  • Preserves deleted files, metadata, slack space, and timestamps
  • Hash of copy matches hash of original
  • Required for legal proceedings
  • Created with dd, FTK Imager, or similar tools

Logical Copy (File-Level)

  • Copies files and folders only
  • Does NOT preserve deleted files or drive structure
  • Hashing a file may match, but a forensic copy hash will differ
  • May be inadmissible in legal proceedings
  • Created with standard copy operations

When in doubt, always create a forensic (bit-for-bit) copy. A logical copy loses critical evidence.

Forensic Acquisition Tools

dd (Linux)

Command-line bit-for-bit copy

dd if=/dev/sda of=image.img conv=noerror,sync

Pipe through tee and md5sum for simultaneous hashing

FTK Imager

Free GUI tool. Multiple format support.

  • Raw, E01 (EnCase), AFF formats
  • Physical and logical drives
  • Memory capture
  • Auto MD5 + SHA1 verification
  • Built-in evidence documentation

WinHex

Disk editor for raw/hex analysis

  • Raw disk images
  • Direct memory access
  • RAID arrays, filesystems
  • Hex-level data reading

Write Blockers

Hardware or software that prevents any writes to the evidence drive. Essential for legally admissible forensic work.

Hash Validation

Hash both the original and copy (MD5/SHA1). Matching hashes prove the image is identical. Used as checksum to detect later changes.

Network, VM & Container Forensics

Network Forensics

  • Network traffic is ephemeral — must be captured proactively
  • Use packet analyzers like Wireshark for deep analysis
  • Network taps, span ports, and port mirrors send copies of traffic to collection servers
  • If not captured live, fall back on firewall logs, IDS/IPS logs, email server logs, and flow data

VM Forensics

  • VMs run on shared hosts — imaging the entire host captures too much
  • VM snapshots capture the state forensic analysts need
  • Snapshots can be imported into forensic tools for analysis

Container Forensics

  • Containers are ephemeral by design — fewer artifacts than VMs
  • Resources are often shared, making forensic capture challenging
  • Containers can be paused, but returning them to a forensically sound state is difficult
  • Requires additional planning and specialized tools

Data Recovery & Slack Space

How Deletion Works

  • Deleting a file only removes the index entry
  • The actual data remains until overwritten
  • Quick format = index deletion, not wiping
  • Recovery tools find files by headers and metadata
  • Partially overwritten files can still yield recoverable fragments

Slack Space

  • Open space on a drive after files are deleted
  • Contains remnants of previously stored data
  • Critical for forensic analysis — reveals historical activity
  • Secure delete tools overwrite this space to prevent recovery

SSD & Flash Media Challenges

Wear leveling moves data to less-worn cells, potentially preserving data in reserved space even after "secure" deletion. Full-disk encryption is the most reliable mitigation for SSDs.

Forensic Suites

Autopsy

Open source. Broad capabilities including timeline analysis, file discovery, hash matching, and module-based processing.

FTK (Forensic Toolkit)

Commercial standard. Comprehensive acquisition, analysis, and reporting. Used in law enforcement and enterprise.

EnCase

Commercial standard. E01 format originator. Extensive analysis, reporting, and court-tested workflows.

Common Capabilities

  • Import disk images, raw disks, VM snapshots, logical files
  • Timeline analysis — see when filesystem changes and events occurred
  • Automatic image/document/artifact discovery
  • Hash cracking, steganographic detection, encryption identification
  • Relies on accurate timestamps — incorrect time settings can mislead investigators

Documentation, Provenance & Reporting

Provenance & Documentation

  • Record where every image/drive came from
  • Document tools used, processes followed, assumptions made
  • Include case numbers, examiner details, notes
  • Prevents repudiation of the forensic process
  • Manual notes and photos supplement tool-generated records

Forensic Reports

  • Summary of investigation and findings
  • Outline of process, tools, and assumptions
  • Detailed findings per device/drive with evidence
  • Recommendations and conclusions
  • Must be useful without overwhelming technical detail
  • Accuracy is critical — may be challenged in court

Cloud Forensics Challenges

Acquiring forensic data directly from a cloud provider is often impossible. You must plan ahead and rely on alternative data sources.

Right-to-Audit Clauses

Negotiate audit rights in contracts. Large providers may only offer third-party audit reports instead.

Regulatory / Jurisdiction

Data may be stored in different countries with different laws. Local authorities may claim access rights.

Breach Notification

Laws vary by jurisdiction. Some vendors delay notification for days, weeks, or months. Ensure your contract addresses this.

Alternative Data Sources

Use your own logs, VM snapshots, and cloud-native archiving tools (Google Vault, etc.) since provider-side forensics is usually unavailable.

Digital Forensics & Intelligence

Beyond legal cases and IR, digital forensics plays a role in strategic intelligence and counterintelligence.

Intelligence Uses

  • Analyze adversary tools, techniques, and behaviors
  • Study APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) components
  • Recover data from acquired systems and devices

Counterintelligence Uses

  • Examine artifacts left behind by adversaries
  • Requires advanced methods: breaking encryption, analyzing hardware
  • Systems may be designed to resist forensic techniques

The exam won't quiz you on specific intelligence tools, but remember that forensic techniques are used in both intelligence and counterintelligence communities.

Exam Tips

1

Know the six IR phases cold. Be ready to identify which phase a scenario describes.

2

Distinguish isolation (remove), containment (restrict in place), and segmentation (separate into zones).

3

Tabletop = discussion. Simulation = active testing. Both are IR exercises.

4

SIEM aggregates and correlates logs. Alert tuning prevents alert fatigue. SOAR automates response.

5

Order of volatility: CPU registers (most) → backups (least). Capture volatile data first.

6

Chain of custody must be maintained throughout. Forensic copies (bit-for-bit) — not logical copies.

7

A legal hold means preserve everything. Spoliation = severe legal consequences.
